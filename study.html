Study & Exam Strategies

Habits that matter more than memorizing formulas.

Every Problem, Same Process

1. Read the whole question. Circle keywords: constant speed, frictionless, elastic, maximum, at rest.
2. Sketch. Label knowns, unknowns, and a positive direction.
3. Free-body diagram if forces matter.
4. Name the principle: Newton 2, energy, momentum, kinematics, …
5. Equations symbolically first.
6. Substitute numbers with units.
7. Check: units, sign, limiting cases, reasonable magnitude.

Which Tool When

Kinematics — constant acceleration, need time/position/velocity, forces not required.
Newton’s laws — forces known or needed; free-body diagram first.
Energy — speeds at different heights; unknown constraint forces that do no work; messy paths.
Momentum — collisions, explosions, brief forces; isolated system.
Circular — net force toward center = mv²/r; identify real forces providing it.

High-Frequency Mistakes

Watch for these

• Distance vs displacement; speed vs velocity
• Cancelling action-reaction pairs on one FBD
• Treating “centripetal force” as an extra force
• Using KE conservation on inelastic collisions
• Setting static friction = μN when not at maximum
• N = mg on inclines or in elevators
• Current “used up” in a resistor
• Magnetic force doing work
• Mixing open-pipe and closed-pipe harmonics
• Sign errors from inconsistent coordinate choices

Study Habits That Work

• Explain out loud (to a person, pet, or wall). Gaps show up fast.
• Translate every problem: words ↔ diagram ↔ equations ↔ graph.
• Keep an error log: wrong answer → what principle was misused.
• Steady weekly practice beats cramming.
• Do not memorize formulas in isolation — attach each to a situation and a diagram.
• After solving, ask: what if mass doubled? friction vanished? angle → 0?

Exam-Day

• Sketch first, even if the rubric does not require it.
• Write units on every intermediate quantity.
• If stuck, name the conserved quantity or the net force direction.
• Multiple choice: estimate order of magnitude before calculating.
• Free response: partial credit follows clear reasoning — show the principle, not only the number.
