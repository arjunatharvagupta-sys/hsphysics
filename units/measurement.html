01 — Measurement & Uncertainty

The foundation of all physics: how we measure, how precise we can be, and how errors work.

Significant Figures

ELI5

When you measure something, you can only be as careful as your tool lets you. Significant figures are the digits that actually mean something — the ones you measured for real, not the ones you just guessed or added because the calculator spit them out.

High School

Significant figures (sig figs) tell us which digits in a measurement are reliable. All non-zero digits are significant. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant. Leading zeros are never significant. Trailing zeros are significant only if a decimal point is present. When multiplying or dividing, the answer has the same number of sig figs as the measurement with the fewest sig figs. When adding or subtracting, the answer is limited by the least precise decimal place.

Professional

Significant figures quantify the precision of a measured quantity. The number of significant figures is determined by the resolution of the measuring instrument and the uncertainty inherent in the measurement process. Rules for propagation of significant figures under arithmetic operations ensure that calculated results do not imply greater precision than the input data support. In rigorous work, uncertainty is expressed explicitly (e.g., 12.34 ± 0.05 cm) rather than relying solely on significant-figure conventions.

Quick Rules Non-zero digits → always significant Zeros between non-zeros → significant Leading zeros → not significant Trailing zeros with decimal → significant × or ÷ → fewest sig figs wins + or − → least precise decimal place wins

Worked Example

You measure length = 12.50 cm and width = 3.2 cm. Area = ?

12.50 has 4 sig figs. 3.2 has 2 sig figs. Area = 40 cm² (2 sig figs).

Common Mistakes

• Counting leading zeros as significant (0.0045 has only 2 sig figs).
• Keeping calculator digits that the measurements don’t support.
• Mixing up the rules for multiplication vs addition.

Accuracy vs Precision

ELI5

Accuracy is hitting the bullseye. Precision is hitting the same spot over and over — even if that spot is nowhere near the bullseye. You can be precise but wrong, or accurate but messy.

High School

Accuracy describes how close a measurement is to the true or accepted value. Precision describes how close repeated measurements are to each other (reproducibility). A set of measurements can be precise but inaccurate (systematic error), accurate but imprecise (large random error), both, or neither.

Professional

Accuracy is the degree of closeness of a measured value to the true value of the measurand. Precision refers to the degree of mutual agreement among independent measurements under stipulated conditions and is quantified by statistical measures such as standard deviation or variance. Systematic errors primarily degrade accuracy; random errors primarily degrade precision. Both must be characterized for a complete uncertainty analysis.

Uncertainty & Error

ELI5

No measurement is perfect. Uncertainty is the “plus or minus” that tells you how much the real value might wiggle around the number you wrote down. Error is the difference between what you got and the real answer.

High School

Absolute uncertainty is the ± amount attached to a measurement (e.g., 25.0 ± 0.1 cm). Relative (percent) uncertainty is (absolute uncertainty / measured value) × 100%. Random errors scatter measurements around the true value; systematic errors shift all measurements in one direction. Percent error = |(experimental − accepted) / accepted| × 100%.

Professional

Measurement uncertainty is a non-negative parameter characterizing the dispersion of values that could reasonably be attributed to the measurand. It is typically expressed as a standard uncertainty (Type A or Type B evaluation) or expanded uncertainty with a stated coverage factor. Error is the difference between a measured result and the true value; because the true value is never known exactly, error itself cannot be known exactly and is instead bounded by the uncertainty.

Percent uncertainty = (Δx / x) × 100% Percent error = |(x_exp − x_true) / x_true| × 100%

SI Units & Scientific Notation

ELI5

Scientists all agreed on the same measuring sticks so everyone talks the same language. Meters for distance, kilograms for mass, seconds for time. Scientific notation is just a short way to write really big or really tiny numbers without a million zeros.

High School

The SI base units used in physics are: meter (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, second (s) for time, ampere (A) for current, kelvin (K) for temperature, mole (mol) for amount of substance, and candela (cd) for luminous intensity. Derived units (newton, joule, watt, etc.) are combinations of these. Scientific notation writes numbers as a × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ |a| < 10.

Professional

The International System of Units (SI) provides a coherent system of base and derived units defined by fixed values of fundamental constants (since the 2019 revision). All derived units are formed by products and quotients of the seven base units. Scientific notation (or standard form) is the conventional representation of numerical values in orders of magnitude, facilitating order-of-magnitude reasoning and consistent significant-figure handling.

Base units: m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd Scientific notation: a × 10ⁿ (1 ≤ |a| < 10)

Dimensional Analysis

ELI5

Units have to match on both sides of an equation, just like you can’t add apples to oranges. If the units don’t work out, the equation is wrong.

High School

Dimensional analysis checks that both sides of an equation have the same base units (dimensions). It is also used to convert between units by multiplying by conversion factors that equal 1. Any valid physics equation must be dimensionally consistent.

Professional

Dimensional homogeneity is a necessary (though not sufficient) condition for the validity of any physical equation. Every term in an equation must transform identically under a change of units of the base quantities. Buckingham’s π theorem formalizes the construction of dimensionless groups from dimensionally consistent sets of variables.
