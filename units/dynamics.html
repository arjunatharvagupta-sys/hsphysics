Dynamics & Newton’s Laws

Forces, free-body diagrams, friction, springs, systems, and the problem-solving habits that prevent most mistakes.

Force and Net Force

Newton’s First Law (Inertia)

Common misconception

“A force is needed to keep something moving.” Everyday friction hides the truth. In the absence of net force, motion continues forever at constant velocity.

Newton’s Second Law

ΣF = ma ΣFₓ = maₓ    ΣFᵧ = maᵧ

Non-negotiable habit

Draw a free-body diagram before writing any equation. Isolate the object, draw every force, label magnitudes and directions, then write Newton’s second law.

Newton’s Third Law

Common mistake

Canceling action-reaction pairs on one free-body diagram. Those two forces act on different objects; only forces on the chosen object belong on its diagram.

Weight, Normal Force, Tension

W = mg

Common mistake

Assuming N = mg always. On an incline N = mg cosθ. In an elevator N can be larger or smaller than mg. N is not “the reaction to weight”; it is determined by the surface constraint.

Friction

fₛ ≤ μₛ N fₖ = μₖ N

Tip

Static friction can be anything from zero up to its maximum. Do not automatically set fₛ = μₛN unless the object is on the verge of slipping or the problem states that it is.

Spring Force (Hooke’s Law)

F_s = −kx PE_s = ½kx²

Systems and Center of Mass

Strategy

If two objects interact (string, contact force) and you do not need that force, put both in one system so the internal forces cancel. If you need the force, isolate one object.

Problem-Solving Checklist

Every mechanics problem

1. Read fully; note keywords (constant speed, frictionless, at rest, maximum). 2. Sketch and choose a positive direction / coordinate system. 3. Draw a free-body diagram for each object you will analyze. 4. Resolve forces into components. 5. Write ΣF = ma (or ΣF = 0 for equilibrium). 6. Solve symbolically, then substitute numbers with units. 7. Check units, sign, and whether the magnitude is reasonable.
